Submittal Log Template for Subcontractors

A submittal log is one row per item you have to get approved before you can buy it. Most logs track what was sent and what came back. The ones that keep jobs on time also track when each item has to be on site, and who is holding it right now.

The columns that matter

  • Spec section and item: what it is, in the spec's words, so the reviewer can find it
  • Supplier: who is preparing the product data or shop drawings
  • Schedule activity and need-on-site date: the activity that uses the item, and when it has to be on site to start
  • Lead time and shipping: from the supplier, in days, after approval
  • Submittal due date: worked back from the need-on-site date (see below)
  • Sent date, review status and returned date: approved, approved as noted, revise and resubmit, or rejected
  • Resubmittal count: a second or third round is an early warning, not a footnote
  • PO issued and delivered dates: approval isn't the finish line; the material on site is
  • Ball in court: who has to act next: the architect, the GC, the supplier or you

Work the dates back from the schedule

A due date that comes from the schedule is one you can defend. Start from the activity that uses the material:

  • Need on site = the activity's start date, minus a few days of buffer for receiving and staging.
  • Order by = need on site, minus the supplier's lead time and shipping.
  • Submittal due = order by, minus the reviewer's turnaround, minus time for one resubmittal.

If the submittal due date is already in the past when you set up the row, you know on day one that the item needs a call, not a reminder. Our guide to figuring a submittal due date from the schedule walks through a full example.

Know who has the ball

Every open row is waiting on someone. Write it down. When the weekly meeting asks where the switchgear stands, "with the engineer since the 3rd, due back the 17th" is a better answer than "submitted." It also shows you quickly which items are stuck on your side: supplier data you haven't sent, or comments you haven't answered.

A five-minute weekly routine

  • Sort by submittal due date and look at anything due in the next two weeks.
  • Check every row where the ball is with you or your supplier, and move it.
  • Follow up on anything past the reviewer's turnaround time.
  • Update the need-on-site dates when the schedule moves. A slipped activity can buy you time; a pulled-in one can make an item late overnight.

Flag it before it's late

A simple color rule does most of the work: green when the item is on track, yellow when the submittal due or order-by date is inside a warning window (say ten days), and red once either date has passed without the next step done. A log you can read at a glance is a log people actually open.
