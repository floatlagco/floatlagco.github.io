Construction Proposals for Subcontractors: What to Include

A proposal is more than a number. The price only means something next to what it covers, what it doesn't, and how long it's good for. A one-page proposal that spells those out wins more work and starts fewer arguments.

What goes on a proposal

  • Your company block. Name, address, phone, email and license number, on letterhead so it looks like you've done this for years.
  • Client and project. Who it's for (the GC or owner), the contact, the project name and the address.
  • The documents you priced. Drawing set and date, spec sections, and any addenda. If the drawings change, this line is how you show your price was for the old set.
  • Scope. A short description of the work in plain words.
  • Includes. What your price covers: material, labor, equipment, delivery, cleanup of your own debris.
  • Excludes. What it doesn't: permits, patching and painting, after-hours work, bonds, testing, disposal of others' debris. Most scope fights start with something nobody wrote down.
  • Price. One clear total, or a base bid plus clearly labeled alternates.
  • Schedule assumptions. Lead times, how many mobilizations you priced, and that the work area will be ready when you arrive.
  • Payment terms. Progress billing, retainage, and when payment is due.
  • Valid until. A date after which the price can change. Material prices move; 30 days is common.
  • Acceptance. A signature and date line for the client.

Price it from line items, not a gut number

Build the price from cost lines: quantity times unit cost for material, hours times rate for labor, plus equipment and subs. Add your markup on top. When the job is won, those same lines become your budget, and when a change comes in, you price it the same way.
